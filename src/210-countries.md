## Countries

Countries do not disappear in 2120, but they matter less in the way they mattered for most of modern history. A border used to decide too much at once: whether you were safe, whether you could work, whether you could afford healthcare, whether you could move, and often whether strangers saw you as fully human. When material survival depends heavily on local wages and local institutions, nationality becomes a high-stakes lottery.

That pressure weakens in the world imagined in this book. If clean energy is abundant, if automation supplies much of what people need, and if a basic income or basic services floor exists across much of the planet, then crossing a border is no longer primarily a desperate search for survival. People still move, but more often for climate, relationships, study, culture, landscape, or a different rhythm of life. The emotional meaning of migration changes when it is less tied to fear.

This does not mean every place becomes identical. Cities still have different languages, habits, architecture, climates and political temperaments. Some regions are denser and more experimental; others are quieter and more traditional. What fades is the old idea that political borders must divide humanity into separate moral camps. In 2120, people are more likely to ask, "Where do I belong for this stage of life?" than "Which nation is my permanent container?"

Mira feels this shift in a practical way. Her legal identity is portable across several regions, so when her partner spends a season working with a marine restoration team on another coast, the family can relocate without starting life from zero. Their health records, education access, transport permissions and housing reputation travel with them. The move is still disruptive—new neighbours, new routines, a child learning local customs—but it is not a bureaucratic cliff.

For borders to soften, governance has to improve rather than vanish. Places still need ways to manage housing demand, ecological limits, security threats and democratic choice. A city-hub cannot simply absorb unlimited newcomers overnight without strain. So the future is more likely to involve layered membership: broad movement rights across large regions, plus local residency systems that pace access to scarce space and infrastructure. That may sound less romantic than "a borderless world," but it is more believable and probably fairer.

There is also a cultural tension here. When people move easily, some communities will fear becoming interchangeable, or becoming playgrounds for wealthier outsiders, or losing the customs that gave them shape. That fear should not be mocked. A humane future has to protect freedom of movement and the right of places to preserve a character worth arriving to in the first place. The goal is not to erase difference. The goal is to make difference less dangerous.

In that sense, countries become lighter and more administrative, while belonging becomes more chosen and more local. People may still feel deep affection for a region, a language, a landscape or a history. But nationality stops being the main gatekeeper of dignity. It becomes one layer of identity among many, instead of a wall around opportunity.
