# Life in hundred years

We live in difficult times. I am writing this in 2021, in the middle of the COVID-19 pandemic. In moments like this, looking a hundred years ahead is not escapism. It is a way to recover perspective, courage and direction. The future is not something that simply arrives. It is something people build, choice by choice, starting now.

Everything humans create is imagined before it is made. A bridge exists in the architect’s mind before it stands over the river. A factory is planned before it bakes a single loaf of bread. A book is written in thought before it reaches the page. Our images of the future matter because they quietly shape what we consider possible, desirable and worth working toward.

This book starts from a simple premise: if energy becomes abundant and clean, and if automation takes over most necessary production, then survival no longer has to dominate everyday life. Food can be grown with far less human labor. Homes can be built faster and cheaper. Transport, healthcare and basic services can become reliable enough that a decent life is not permanently tied to a job. When survival is gradually decoupled from labor, society reorganizes around different questions.

Then the center of life shifts. Instead of asking, “How do I earn enough to stay safe?” more people can ask, “How do I grow? How do I contribute? What kind of relationships do I want? What kind of life is meaningful?” That is the governing assumption behind the chapters that follow. This manuscript imagines a world where scarcity loses its grip, and human development, curiosity and connection move closer to the center.

So this is partly predictive and partly normative. Some pages extrapolate trends that are already visible: cheaper energy, smarter machines, automation, remote work, new forms of education and production. Other pages describe the future we should want to build because it is more humane, less fearful and less wasteful. I am not trying to produce a cold academic forecast. I am trying to sketch a believable and hopeful direction.

Let us imagine, then, that these ideas mature over the next century and become ordinary reality. What would daily life feel like in 2120? How would money, work, education, cities, travel, laws and relationships change? Let’s explore that world together.

## A human frame

Futuristic writing easily becomes too abstract. So let us keep one ordinary person in mind while reading the next chapters.

Her name is Mira. She is 36 years old and lives in a compact city-hub with her partner and one school-age child. She is not rich, famous or unusually technical. She is simply a resident of 2120 with access to the systems this book talks about.

When the book discusses energy, money, health, education or travel, the useful question is simple: what changes in Mira’s Tuesday? If a future idea does not improve an ordinary day for her, it is probably not a meaningful improvement.
## Energy

We can describe a civilisation, and often an individual life, by the amount of reliable energy available to it. For millions of years human power meant muscle: arms, legs, endurance. Being strong, fast and physically resilient was not a lifestyle preference. It was survival.

With the domestication of animals, we widened that power base. Horses, oxen and other animals let us move goods, plough fields and travel farther than muscle alone allowed. Then, for long stretches of history, societies also forced other humans to provide energy through slavery, servitude or poorly paid labour. Grand temples and empires were not only ideas or beliefs. They were concentrated power in a literal sense.

Machines changed the scale again. Gears, levers and engines allowed people to use stored energy instead of immediate muscle. That is why the steam engine marked such a sharp break with the past. For the first time, large amounts of work could be done without depending directly on human or animal bodies.

Electricity expanded that break to an extraordinary degree. One horsepower is 746 watts. A typical home in Germany can use about 7,360 watts through a 230V, 32A connection. That is roughly the output of ten horses, quietly available in an apartment wall socket. What once required land, fodder and stable hands now arrives invisibly through infrastructure.

The meaning of energy changes too. In 1900, much of electricity went toward lighting and heavy machinery. In the early twenty-first century, turning on a light is barely an event at all. Energy is already becoming less visible at the point of use even while it remains one of the deepest constraints underneath modern life.

As a humanity we use around 120,000 TWh per year, and a large share still comes from fossil fuels. That fact matters because it reveals both our dependence and our bottleneck: modern civilisation already runs on enormous energy flows, but too much of that power is dirty, finite or geopolitically fragile.

- - - -
Now imagine the year 2120. Fusion power has been practical for decades, alongside mature solar, storage and other clean systems. Cheap, clean electricity removes many barriers to human activity. Providing power to every region on Earth becomes ordinary rather than heroic. Large infrastructure projects become feasible: desalinating water at scale, pulling carbon from the atmosphere, cooling overheated regions, recycling difficult materials, perhaps even pumping water into places that were once written off as permanently dry.

For Mira this is not an engineering headline but a daily background condition. Her flat stays warm without financial anxiety. The shared workshop downstairs can run power-hungry tools. The local vertical farm does not make fresh food expensive simply because lighting and climate control are affordable. Energy becomes more like clean water: expected, reliable and woven into ordinary life.

Still, abundant energy is not a magic wand. It can make many solutions affordable, but it does not automatically decide who governs them, who benefits first or which landscapes should remain untouched. Cheap power does not remove the need for judgment. It only means humanity can spend less effort fighting raw scarcity and more effort choosing wisely.

That shift matters for the rest of this book. Abundant energy does not merely keep machines running. It lowers the cost of housing, transport, food production, health systems and climate repair. In other words, it weakens the old bond between survival and exhausting labour. Once the energetic foundation changes, social life begins to reorganise above it.
## Climate restoration

Cheap clean energy is not only about comfort. It is also about repair. By 2120, humanity should be judged not by whether it managed to slow the damage a little, but by whether it learned to restore the systems it had destabilised. Survival on a degraded planet is too small an ambition.

I do not expect nature to heal automatically just because we feel guilty. Forests can recover if protected, wetlands can be rebuilt and species can sometimes return, but only through deliberate long-term work. The climate problem is not a single switch. It is a web of carbon in the air, heat in the oceans, damaged soils, acidifying waters, disappearing habitats and political habits formed around extraction.

This is where abundant energy changes the picture. Direct air capture, desalination, recycled materials, synthetic fuels for the few sectors that still need them, large-scale ecological monitoring and climate-resilient construction all become more feasible when electricity is cheap and clean. But technology should not become an excuse for arrogance. The first task is still to stop making the problem worse. There is no wisdom in capturing carbon with one machine while ten other industries keep burning and dumping because nobody wanted to challenge their business model.

The restoration century would probably create huge public works. Rivers are given room to flood safely instead of being trapped between concrete walls. Coastlines are redesigned with marshes, reefs and elevated settlements. Former highways are converted into green corridors. Industrial agriculture gives way, in part, to regenerative methods and vertical farms that free land for ecosystems. Some regions may even become more beautiful than they were in 2020, not because humans vanished, but because humans finally learned to design with humility.

There will still be loss. Some cities will retreat from coastlines. Some species will not come back. Some communities will spend generations adapting to damage they did little to cause. Optimism that ignores this is childish. Real optimism means believing repair is possible while admitting the bill is enormous.

I hope that by 2120 children will grow up with a different default attitude: the atmosphere, soil and ocean are not a backdrop to the economy. They are the primary inheritance. A civilisation that can restore climate systems is not merely richer. It is older in character.
## Money

Let us take the idea of universal basic income seriously and ask what it does to everyday life. The central change is simple: nobody is required to sell their labour every week merely to remain housed, fed and safe. That is not a small policy adjustment. It is a deep psychological break with most of human history.

For animals, and then for humans, the main concern was survival: find food, secure shelter, protect resources, endure winter, avoid exclusion. In modern economies, money became the abstract measure of access to those necessities. People with more money could secure more comfort, more options and more buffers against disaster. People with too little lived under constant pressure.

That pressure shapes inner life. We go to work not only because work can be meaningful, but because refusing work often means risking humiliation, debt or dependency. Losing a job becomes more than an economic event. It becomes a threat to identity, status and safety. Even people who like their work often carry a quieter fear underneath it: the need to be faster, more productive, more employable than the next person.

It is therefore not surprising that so much of modern anxiety attaches to labour. A society that ties dignity to employment also turns exhaustion into a moral test.

- - - -
In 2120 this old arrangement loosens. Energy is abundant, food is grown with far less human labour, homes are built more cheaply, and much transport and maintenance is automated. Basic income, or some functional equivalent, becomes realistic because the material cost of keeping a person decently alive is far lower than it was in earlier centuries.

This changes Mira’s inner life more than her shopping habits. She can spend a month helping at a local learning centre, recovering from burnout or caring for her child without the old fear of “falling behind” and becoming poor. Survival is no longer attached to constant employment, so dignity is no longer rationed by the labour market.

Millions of people would feel enormous psychological relief under such a system. Being a human being is enough to justify food, shelter, healthcare and a modest but decent standard of living. That may sound obvious morally, but historically it has been radical in practice.

Of course, money does not disappear overnight, and inequality does not vanish just because desperation recedes. People will still want scarce things: beautiful locations, rare experiences, prestige, influence, exceptional craftsmanship. Some will build companies, invent new tools, make art, teach, compete and accumulate reputation. The difference is that these ambitions no longer sit on top of a survival panic.

Would this reduce competition and produce stagnation? Perhaps some forms of competition would soften, especially the cruel and wasteful kind driven by fear. But curiosity, status, pride, generosity and the desire to contribute would remain powerful motives. In fact, once routine labour is less compulsory, the more interesting question becomes not “Why would anyone do anything?” but “What kinds of work are truly worth doing when compulsion weakens?”

That is where this chapter connects to automation, education and meaning. When machines do more necessary work, humans are pushed toward different forms of value: care, judgment, invention, culture, mentorship and local problem-solving. Money matters less as a permission slip to exist and more as one coordination tool among many in a richer social world.
## Education

Everybody knows that the education was invented by factory owners who needed trained workers. Our education 100 years later still bears the same design principles. Everybody is learning the same subjects, in the same pace regardless of their abilities and natural talents. The study time is split into equal classes 45 minutes long. Any differences in children are neglected or treated as deviation that needs punishment. It more seems like the education for robots than people.

I hope it would change in 2120. First of all, we need smaller classes. One teacher is unable to attend to needs and differences of 30 pupils at the same time as teaching them a lesson. A perfect class size should be determined experimentally, I would recommend to start with 10 pupils. A teacher should become a mentor. Teaching the exact scientific material should be only 1/3 of the duties. The rest should be a development supervision, knowledge nourishment and psychological development.

No wonder our schools produce mediocre people in droves. Thin about it - every child has a potential to become great. But only if this potential is recognised and cherished. Current schools do nothing of the kind. It’s a shame we deprive our society of the best people.

## Automation

One more reason for not treating our children as future factory workers is this. All work that can be done by robots will be done by robots. This is logical - even most expensive robots are cheaper in the long term than humans with their needs for variety, novelty, food, meaning in life, labor unions, maximal working hours and minimal salary. We are living in the transition zone from human work to machine work. This transition looks scary for 3 million truck drivers in the U.S. But it’s inevitable, just like we don’t grow each our own food (farmers do it more efficiently), we don’t build our own house (building company is way more efficient and experienced). The same way we will not do jobs that can be done by machine or AI. It’s just counter-productive.

So we don’t need to train our children as workers. We should develop their higher qualities: fascination with life, desire to help others, life-long self-education, creativity, problem-solving, entrepreneurship, healthy diet, natural talents.

## Work from home

For most of the industrial era, work was a place you had to travel to. Offices, factories and campuses concentrated tools, records and authority in one physical location, and workers organized their lives around that fact. The daily commute became so normal that whole cities were built to serve it, even though it consumed enormous time, energy and attention.

By 2120, much of that logic weakens. Communication is immediate, shared digital environments feel far more embodied than today’s video calls, and many routine tasks are automated anyway. For a large share of knowledge, design, coordination and care work, the home becomes the default base. Not because people become antisocial, but because presence is finally optional instead of mandatory.

That change gives back more than travel time. It changes the emotional structure of a day. Mira does not begin every morning by calculating traffic, packing for transit and surrendering two tired hours to movement she never wanted. She starts with breakfast at home, helps her child prepare for the day, and joins a collaborative studio from a quiet room when she is ready. The technology matters, but the deeper change is that daily life is no longer arranged around proving physical attendance.

Still, "work from home" is not the whole story. Some activities remain better in person: building hardware, repairing infrastructure, practicing medicine, performing, celebrating, mentoring or making difficult decisions that benefit from full human presence. Even digital work gains something from occasional shared rooms. So the future is less an endless home office than a wider menu of workplaces: home for focus, neighbourhood work lounges for collaboration, specialized labs for physical tasks, and civic venues for gatherings that actually deserve a journey.

This flexibility also changes who benefits from cities. If people do not need to crowd into expensive central districts five days a week, housing pressure eases and smaller city-hubs become more attractive. Parents, disabled people, older adults and anyone whose life does not fit the old commuter template gain room to participate on better terms. A society that reduces forced commuting is not merely more efficient; it is more forgiving of ordinary human life.

But there is a trade-off. The home can become liberating or oppressive depending on how it is designed. A calm private room is very different from a cramped apartment shared by several people. Without care, remote work can blur boundaries until one is always vaguely on duty. It can also deepen isolation for those who need social contact or live alone. That is why the future chapter on cities matters here: if work moves home, homes and neighbourhoods must become more supportive, with quiet shared workrooms, childcare options and public places that prevent domestic life from becoming a soft prison.

In 2120 the best version of work from home is therefore not an employer saving rent. It is a society deciding that human time has value beyond productivity. When people travel less for routine obligation, they can travel more intentionally—for collaboration, celebration, study or love. The point is not to keep everyone indoors. The point is to stop wasting life on unnecessary transit.
## Transportation

Transportation changes in 2120 for the same reason work changes: society no longer organizes movement around millions of people making the same trip at the same hour. Once commuting declines, the whole system can be redesigned around convenience, safety and access rather than daily traffic survival.

The most visible change is that driving becomes rare. Autonomous vehicles are ordinary, and most of them are not privately owned. Instead of every household storing an expensive machine that sits idle most of the day, city-hubs rely on shared fleets that arrive when needed, route efficiently and coordinate with public transit. Private cars survive in some rural areas, for enthusiasts, and for special uses, but they no longer define the shape of streets.

That frees an astonishing amount of space. Parking lots shrink. Roads narrow where high speed is no longer necessary. Deliveries happen in quieter, better-timed windows. Streets that once felt like mechanical rivers become mixed public space again: trees, bike lanes, walking routes, play areas, loading zones, and small autonomous shuttles moving at predictable speeds. The city feels less like an obstacle course and more like a habitat.

Mira barely thinks about transport on an ordinary day, which is precisely the point. She cycles to the market in ten minutes, calls a shared vehicle when rain makes that unpleasant, and books a longer autonomous ride when visiting relatives in another district. Her child can travel independently much earlier than children in car-dominated cities today because the streets are calmer and the transport system is legible. Mobility becomes less stressful because it becomes less dramatic.

Public transit does not disappear in this future; it becomes more useful. High-capacity trains and trams are still the most sensible way to move many people through dense corridors. Automation mainly makes them cleaner, more frequent and easier to coordinate with everything else. Freight also improves. Goods move in synchronized networks that waste less energy and less curb space, which matters in a world where local food, medical supplies and manufactured items arrive continuously rather than through giant weekly shopping trips.

The tension is resilience and control. When movement depends on software, networks and shared systems, breakdowns can affect many people at once. A transport system that knows where everyone is going can also become a surveillance system if laws and governance are weak. And some people will resist the loss of manual control, seeing human driving as freedom rather than risk. Those objections are not trivial. The future only feels better if automated transport is accountable, hack-resistant and easy to override when necessary.

So transportation in 2120 is not only about smarter vehicles. It is about requiring fewer pointless trips, making necessary trips gentler, and returning public space to human use. We may still keep the equivalent of horse stables for hobby driving and nostalgia. But the ordinary transport system will feel less like a contest for road space and more like an invisible public service.
## Cities

Living in a multi-million-person city made sense when huge numbers of people had to travel daily to concentrated workplaces. Offices, ministries, factories, hospitals, universities and financial districts all pulled people toward the same centre. Under those conditions, dense cities were efficient even when they were stressful.

But if work becomes more distributed, that old urban logic weakens. Long commutes start to look less like progress and more like a tax on life. House prices remain high because too many people are competing for proximity to places they no longer need to visit every day. Air quality suffers. Traffic swallows time. A choice of two hundred restaurants does not compensate for exhaustion.

Would it not be better to live in a smaller community? Not a nostalgic village with poor infrastructure and limited opportunity, but a compact city-hub with the technical quality of a major metropolis and the daily scale of a neighbourhood.

Imagine a town where top-class services are reachable by bicycle. Bike lanes are separated from roads. Districts are connected by green corridors rather than chopped apart by parking lots and traffic noise. Children can move more independently. Older people are not trapped by car dependence. Daily life becomes physically smaller without becoming culturally narrow.

The concentration of everything in one centre also becomes less necessary. Historically, centralisation was efficient because cities grew ad hoc around transport bottlenecks and employment clusters. But if urban layout is planned in advance, each district can have a modest centre with most essential services: healthcare, food, education, entertainment, repair and a library of things.

City centres, in that future, would not be dominated by office towers. There would still be specialised hubs for research, surgery, large performances, manufacturing, governance and events that benefit from scale. But most daily needs would be met locally, and most people would travel to a larger centre by choice rather than obligation.

Mira feels this scale every day. Her child’s mentor, the health clinic, the food market and the library of things are all within a short bicycle ride. The city is no longer experienced as a machine that must be crossed in order to function. It becomes a place that gives back time.

This vision should not be romanticised. Good urban life still requires planning discipline, maintenance, housing policy, water systems, public safety and political competence. Density can still produce conflict. Some people will prefer great metropolitan centres, and some industries will still need them. The point is not that big cities disappear, but that daily urban life becomes less organised around scarcity, commuting and real-estate panic.

That change links directly to the book’s larger thesis. When labour is less central, cities no longer need to be designed mainly as labour-delivery systems. They can be designed around health, access, sociability and ordinary human time.
## Travel

When movement between regions becomes easier and daily work becomes less tied to one building, travel changes from a narrow luxury into a normal part of life. Not constant travel for everyone, and not a planet where every place turns into an airport lounge, but a world in which spending part of a year elsewhere becomes achievable for ordinary people.

Today travel is often compressed, expensive and slightly absurd. We save for a short escape, rush through unfamiliar places, take photos to prove we were there, then return to the same routine exhausted. In 2120 travel is more likely to stretch out. If housing can be booked through interoperable residency networks, if personal settings and records follow you securely, and if much routine work or learning can continue from anywhere, then a trip can become a season rather than a weekend.

Mira uses this possibility in a modest way. Her family spends part of one summer in a cooler northern city-hub while her child joins a marine ecology program there. Their apartment back home is not sitting empty in wasteful isolation; it flows into a broader housing exchange system, more like a well-regulated subscription than a second-home economy. They are not performing wealth. They are temporarily relocating life.

That kind of travel has educational value. People who spend meaningful time in other places tend to discover that convenience, beauty and good governance can be arranged differently. Food tastes different. Streets sound different. Neighbours disagree politely in unfamiliar ways. The world becomes harder to stereotype once it has been lived in rather than consumed quickly. A more mobile society could therefore become a less provincial one.

Yet hyper-mobility has costs. A city that is always welcoming newcomers may also become harder for locals to afford or harder to emotionally stabilize. If everyone can leave whenever life feels difficult, commitment may weaken. Tourism can flatten culture into performance, and long-distance movement still consumes energy and infrastructure even in a cleaner age. The optimistic future is not one in which people are endlessly in transit, but one in which travel is common without turning belonging into a disposable product.

That balance probably requires new norms. Some places will cap short-term stays. Some housing will be reserved primarily for long-term residents. Slow rail and clean regional transport may replace a portion of the old aviation frenzy. The technology of access matters, but so does etiquette: visitors have to arrive as temporary participants, not entitled consumers.

The best travel future is therefore not about escape. It is about permeability. People can go farther more easily, stay long enough to learn something, and return without rebuilding their lives from scratch. Travel becomes one more way of expanding a human life rather than one more market for status.
## Countries

Countries do not disappear in 2120, but they matter less in the way they mattered for most of modern history. A border used to decide too much at once: whether you were safe, whether you could work, whether you could afford healthcare, whether you could move, and often whether strangers saw you as fully human. When material survival depends heavily on local wages and local institutions, nationality becomes a high-stakes lottery.

That pressure weakens in the world imagined in this book. If clean energy is abundant, if automation supplies much of what people need, and if a basic income or basic services floor exists across much of the planet, then crossing a border is no longer primarily a desperate search for survival. People still move, but more often for climate, relationships, study, culture, landscape, or a different rhythm of life. The emotional meaning of migration changes when it is less tied to fear.

This does not mean every place becomes identical. Cities still have different languages, habits, architecture, climates and political temperaments. Some regions are denser and more experimental; others are quieter and more traditional. What fades is the old idea that political borders must divide humanity into separate moral camps. In 2120, people are more likely to ask, "Where do I belong for this stage of life?" than "Which nation is my permanent container?"

Mira feels this shift in a practical way. Her legal identity is portable across several regions, so when her partner spends a season working with a marine restoration team on another coast, the family can relocate without starting life from zero. Their health records, education access, transport permissions and housing reputation travel with them. The move is still disruptive—new neighbours, new routines, a child learning local customs—but it is not a bureaucratic cliff.

For borders to soften, governance has to improve rather than vanish. Places still need ways to manage housing demand, ecological limits, security threats and democratic choice. A city-hub cannot simply absorb unlimited newcomers overnight without strain. So the future is more likely to involve layered membership: broad movement rights across large regions, plus local residency systems that pace access to scarce space and infrastructure. That may sound less romantic than "a borderless world," but it is more believable and probably fairer.

There is also a cultural tension here. When people move easily, some communities will fear becoming interchangeable, or becoming playgrounds for wealthier outsiders, or losing the customs that gave them shape. That fear should not be mocked. A humane future has to protect freedom of movement and the right of places to preserve a character worth arriving to in the first place. The goal is not to erase difference. The goal is to make difference less dangerous.

In that sense, countries become lighter and more administrative, while belonging becomes more chosen and more local. People may still feel deep affection for a region, a language, a landscape or a history. But nationality stops being the main gatekeeper of dignity. It becomes one layer of identity among many, instead of a wall around opportunity.

## Laws

Technology tends to ignore jurisdictional boundaries long before governments do. Data crosses continents instantly. Autonomous vehicles move through several legal systems in a day. Medical platforms, payment systems and learning networks serve users in many places at once. In such a world, purely local law becomes too weak for some problems and too clumsy for others.

So by 2120 many laws are likely to become more interoperable, and some may become genuinely global. Not every rule, but the ones that fail badly when fragmented: basic human rights, digital identity standards, data portability, consumer protection, environmental reporting, autonomous-system safety, and crimes that occur across borders or in places that used to confuse jurisdiction. Cases like the old T-3 Arctic killing feel strange to us precisely because they reveal how much justice once depended on arbitrary geography.

A more unified legal layer would make everyday life calmer. Mira does not need to master a new legal universe every time she travels or buys a service from another region. Her medical consent records, housing agreements and work contracts rely on shared standards. If a platform mistreats her, she can appeal through procedures that are legible across jurisdictions instead of disappearing into a maze of terms and conditions.

But a world of shared law should not become a world of one giant rulebook. Local communities still need the power to govern land use, school culture, public rituals, opening hours, noise, architecture, and countless other matters that shape daily life. Law works best in layers: a broad floor beneath everyone, then regional and local variation above it. The goal is not sameness. The goal is compatibility where compatibility protects people.

Enforcement also changes. Digital systems make compliance easier to monitor, contracts easier to verify, and disputes easier to document. That sounds efficient, and often it is. It can also become chilling if every disagreement is instantly legible to an automated authority. A humane legal future must preserve due process, room for appeal, and limits on what can be measured automatically. Otherwise convenience quietly turns into control.

There is a political tension here as well. Global legal standards can protect the weak from local abuses, but they can also feel distant, technocratic and hard to challenge. People accept law more readily when they believe they had a hand in shaping it. So if laws become more unified, democratic participation has to scale with them. A world parliament alone would not be enough; people would need meaningful representation through nested institutions that still feel close to life.

In the optimistic version of 2120, law becomes less arbitrary and less parochial without becoming faceless. It helps people move, cooperate and trust beyond the old national containers, while still leaving room for places to remain themselves. That balance is difficult, but it is more realistic than either fantasy extreme: total legal fragmentation or one perfectly governed planet.
## Governance

If countries become less important, governance does not disappear. It becomes more visible. Somebody still has to maintain water systems, health standards, transport corridors, data rights, environmental limits and emergency response. The fantasy that a highly advanced society can run on pure spontaneity is attractive, but it is still a fantasy. In 2120, government is probably smaller in its rituals and larger in its practical competence.

A lot of political theatre of the 21st century came from scarcity and distance. People voted every few years for strangers who promised impossible things, then those strangers disappeared into institutions nobody understood. I hope this layer becomes thinner. Local decisions should be made locally, where people can see the trade-offs with their own eyes. A town deciding how much land to devote to housing, forests or vertical farming does not need a parliament on another continent. At the same time, some questions are obviously global: atmospheric carbon, ocean protection, AI safety, pandemic monitoring, migration rights. These need shared institutions with real authority, not just conferences and polite declarations.

So governance in 2120 is likely layered. The neighbourhood decides on parks, schools, noise and common spaces. The city-region coordinates transport, hospitals and ecological planning. Global institutions maintain a common legal floor: human rights, access to mobility, resource accounting, scientific standards and conflict mediation. This is less romantic than “a world without government”, but much more humane than thousands of competing bureaucracies.

Digital systems would make this easier, but only if they are designed with suspicion toward power. We will certainly use AI to simulate policy outcomes, detect corruption, translate public debates and process feedback from millions of people. But nobody should be ruled by a black box. Important public algorithms will have to be transparent, inspectable and open to challenge. In the future, civic literacy may include the ability to ask not only “who made this law?” but also “who trained this model, on what data, and who can override it?”

I do not imagine a perfect society where everybody agrees. I imagine one where disagreement is cheaper and less dangerous. When survival is not at stake, politics can become less tribal. People may still argue fiercely about privacy, public aesthetics, child development or the rights of synthetic minds. That is healthy. The sign of progress is not the end of politics. It is the replacement of panic, propaganda and domination with institutions that are calm enough to learn.
## Surveillance

It’s bad news for freedom fighters. You could be on your own in the past if you travel long enough and there would be no supervision. There’s a tendency to impose police, laws and government everywhere. In 2120 you would be under supervision at all times. Your smart home needs to know when you are coming back, your smart kitchen needs to sense when you become hungry, the self-driving car will report all your movements to the server and your AI doctor needs to recommend you a new supplement when it senses your blood composition change. If we want the world to be convenient - we need to provide it with information about us. There’s no way around it.

## Housing and property

Housing is one of the places where our current civilisation looks the most irrational. We know how to build, yet millions are priced out of shelter. We have empty apartments, yet homeless people. We treat land not only as a place to live, but as a financial weapon. It is hard to imagine a mature society in 2120 preserving this contradiction for another century.

If construction is increasingly automated and energy is cheap, the physical act of building a good home becomes much less expensive than it is today. The scarce part is no longer concrete, glass or labour hours. The scarce part is access to attractive land, infrastructure and social trust. That means the politics of housing shifts from “can we build enough?” to “who gets to control the locations everybody wants?”

I think private space will absolutely remain. People need doors they can close, quiet they can trust and a place that reflects their taste. But the meaning of property changes. Owning five apartments while others are locked out will look as primitive as owning a private well in the middle of a drought. Some combination of long-term use rights, community land trusts and public ownership of essential land is more likely. You may have secure rights to your home for decades, improve it, personalise it and even exchange it, without treating the planet underneath as a casino chip.

This would also fit with a more mobile world. Not everybody will want one fixed address for life. Some people will live in one city-hub for ten years; others will rotate between climates, projects or family networks. A mature housing system should allow both stability and movement. There could be a guaranteed base residence for every person, plus a much wider layer of short- and medium-term housing that feels more like public infrastructure than speculative real estate.

The design of homes will matter as much as their price. If work, learning and care happen closer to home, then a good dwelling is not only a sleeping box. It needs acoustic privacy, healthy air, flexible rooms, good connectivity and access to common gardens, workshops and child-friendly public space. In the 20th century we often built for cars and investors first, humans second. In 2120 that order should be reversed.

Property will not disappear, but it will lose some of its sacred aura. We already accept that there are limits to what one person can do with a river, a hospital or an airport. Housing should move into the same category: partly personal, partly social, always embedded in the wider system that makes a home possible at all.
## Food production

There can still be small farms between city-hubs. Much depends on climate, terrain and culture. Some landscapes favour orchards, grains, grazing or open-field agriculture. But in many places the dominant trend will be clear: more food produced in controlled environments, with fewer inputs wasted and far less back-breaking labour.

For millennia, farming expanded mostly across a two-dimensional surface. When more food was needed, more land was cleared. That made sense when energy was limited, construction was expensive and environmental control was crude. But with cheap clean electricity, robotic maintenance and advanced building systems, it often becomes more efficient to grow upward rather than outward.

Vertical farming is compelling for a simple physical reason: control. In an open field, much of the water poured onto crops disappears into the ground or evaporates beyond recovery. Nutrients wash away. Weather destroys work overnight. Pests and birds take their share. In a vertical farm, water can circulate, drip downward through multiple levels and be measured precisely. Fertiliser stays inside the system. Temperature, light and humidity become variables rather than fate.

That does not mean every crop moves indoors. Grains, grazing animals and some fruit will still make sense in open landscapes, and many people will continue to value food tied to soil, region and season. The future of food is not one giant indoor machine replacing every field. It is a more intelligent division of labour between controlled systems and outdoor agriculture.

The advantage is still enormous. Controlled farming can cut water use dramatically, reduce transport distances, lower pesticide needs and make harvests more predictable. The main historical cost was energy: replacing sunlight, pumping water, running sensors and climate systems. But if electricity is abundant in 2120, that constraint weakens.

For cities, the consequences are immediate. Food production moves closer to where people live. Fewer trucks need to cross continents just to deliver fragile produce. Neighbourhood markets can sell fresher vegetables because some of them were harvested that morning a few kilometres away. Supply chains become shorter, cleaner and less vulnerable to droughts or geopolitical shocks.

For labour, the change is just as large. Farming does not disappear, but much of its hardest repetitive work is automated or transformed into monitoring, repair, biology, logistics and design. Fewer human lives are spent doing physically punishing tasks simply because there was no better system.

Mira experiences this not as an abstract efficiency gain but as a calmer daily baseline. Good produce is normal, not seasonal luxury. Food waste is lower. Prices are steadier. Her city still has regional dishes and local taste, but less of the old background fear that climate, transport failure or price spikes will turn nutrition into another source of stress.

So food production becomes one more example of the book’s wider claim: once energy, automation and planning reduce basic scarcity, a decent life requires less anxiety and less compulsory labour. The result is not a fantasy of infinite abundance, but a more resilient and humane metabolism between people, land and machines.
## Library of things

Modern households are full of objects that spend almost all of their lives waiting. A drill may be used for fifteen minutes every few months. Camping gear sits in a closet eleven months of the year. A pressure washer, sewing machine, ladder, projector or barbecue grill occupies space because occasional need is still need. The result is a strange kind of private abundance: millions of people each buying, storing and maintaining underused tools in parallel.

That pattern looks wasteful from almost any angle. It consumes money, materials and housing space. It also quietly raises the cost of a decent life, because "having your own place" often means having to assemble a private warehouse of rarely used objects. In a future where cities are more compact and homes are designed around comfort rather than accumulation, this begins to feel unnecessary.

The library of things is the simple alternative. Instead of owning every low-frequency object, neighbourhoods maintain shared inventories of tools, appliances, hobby equipment, event supplies and repair devices. Some are run publicly like a library, some cooperatively by residents, some by private services with strong standards. The important shift is from ownership to reliable access.

For Mira this is not ideological. It is just convenient. Her apartment stays smaller and calmer because she does not store equipment for every imaginable activity. When friends visit, she books extra tableware and a folding table from the district inventory. When the family plans a weekend outside the city, she reserves camping gear that was cleaned and checked the previous afternoon. When a bike part breaks, she borrows a repair kit instead of buying a drawer full of tools she barely understands.

For the system to work, trust has to be designed in. Objects need maintenance logs, cleaning routines, easy booking, repair budgets and some method of dealing with damage that does not become punitive or humiliating. Shared goods fail when everyone assumes someone else will care for them. They succeed when stewardship is visible and ordinary. In that sense, the library of things is not only a storage solution; it is a small lesson in civic maturity.

There are trade-offs. Access is not quite the same as ownership. Sometimes you want a thing immediately and someone else already has it. Some people prefer personal tools because they know exactly how they were treated. Others may fear that sharing creates another layer of digital tracking or social scoring. Those concerns are real, which is why the best systems leave room for both models: own what is deeply personal or frequently used, share what is occasional and standardized.

If done well, the benefits spread far beyond clutter reduction. Lower material demand means less extraction and less waste. Smaller homes become more viable because they are supported by richer neighbourhood infrastructure. Young people setting up their first households do not need to buy an entire adulthood at once. A community with a strong library of things feels wealthier not because each person owns more, but because useful capability is always nearby.

This is one of the most attractive features of an abundant future: abundance no longer means every individual hoarding a duplicate of everything. It can mean that good tools, like good knowledge, circulate.
## Health

Even in the early twenty-first century, many people already wear devices that collect health data: steps, sleep, pulse, blood oxygen, temperature and more. Yet this information is fragmented across apps, brands and business models. It exists, but not in a way that is well integrated with care.

The deeper problem is not only technical. It is institutional. Doctors are usually paid to respond once a patient shows up with a problem, not to notice a pattern early and prevent that problem from growing. If a person’s sleep quality collapses, stress markers stay high for weeks or blood pressure trends in the wrong direction, the system often does nothing until the situation becomes serious enough to demand an appointment.

That leaves too much responsibility on the individual. Some people ignore symptoms because they are busy, uninformed, ashamed or afraid of cost. Others become hypochondriacs and seek help constantly without clear guidance. In both cases the health system acts late.

- - - -
I hope that in 2120 we will have health systems that monitor the body much more continuously through voluntary sensors, routine scans and AI-assisted pattern recognition. Doctors, nurses and other clinicians would receive warnings when a situation actually deserves attention. Patients would be contacted proactively: not only when something is already wrong, but when a trend suggests it soon could be.

Mira notices the difference when her sleep quality, stress markers and blood data drift in the wrong direction for several days. She does not need to guess whether it matters or postpone action for months. The system flags the pattern, a clinician checks it and she gets help while the problem is still small.

This is not only about catching disease earlier. It changes the philosophy of medicine from episodic repair to prevention. Nutrition, movement, air quality, stress, addiction, reproductive health and mental health can all be treated as part of one continuous picture of a life instead of as disconnected emergencies.

Mental health especially benefits from this shift. A humane future cannot define health only as the absence of visible illness. If people are lonely, chronically anxious, sleep-deprived or burned out, that also belongs inside the health system’s field of concern. In a world less dominated by survival labour, it becomes easier to admit that emotional stability and meaning are public-health issues too.

At the same time, the boundary with surveillance must remain clear. Helpful monitoring should not mean compulsory extraction of intimate data for employers, insurers or states. People should understand what is collected, who can see it and how to opt out. Otherwise a preventive health system could easily become another machinery of control.

So the incentives have to change as well as the tools. We should not reward healthcare systems mainly for processing sick people after the damage is done. We should reward them for keeping people well, independent and informed. Doctors, in that sense, are paid not for the number of crises they witness but for the number of crises that never happen.

That would be a profound improvement in daily life. Health becomes less about waiting until something breaks and more about living in an environment that helps ordinary people stay physically and mentally steady over time.
## Family and children

When survival becomes less stressful, family life can become more honest. A lot of damage in the modern family comes not from lack of love, but from exhaustion. Parents are overworked, children are overscheduled, grandparents are isolated and everybody is negotiating time under pressure. If 2120 is really wealthier in the deep sense, then families should feel less like emergency units and more like places of growth.

I do not think the future produces one standard family model. People will still form couples, large households, single-parent homes, co-parenting arrangements and friendships that function like kinship. What should disappear is the punishment attached to difference. A child needs continuity, affection, safety and competent adults. This matters more than whether the household matches an old template.

Children themselves will likely have much more dignity. In many places we still treat them as a project owned by adults: shape them, discipline them, rank them, prepare them for the labour market. But if automation removes the old pressure to manufacture obedient workers, then childhood can become less instrumental. Education would still matter enormously, yet not as a sorting machine. The aim becomes to notice who the child already is and help that person unfold.

This does not mean permissiveness without boundaries. Children need structure precisely because they are inexperienced. The difference is that the structure should be intelligible and humane. Instead of constant punishment, there would be coaching, mediation and developmental support. If sensors and AI assistants are good enough to track sleep, mood and learning patterns, families might get early help before stress becomes abuse or neglect. That requires strict privacy protections, because the home should not become another site of surveillance for profit.

Longer healthy lifespans will also rearrange generations. Imagine great-grandparents who are active mentors rather than distant memories. Family knowledge, stories and practical care could flow across four or five living generations. At the same time, women in particular should not be trapped by an expanded expectation of unpaid care. A decent society shares the work of raising children through public services, neighbourhood support and generous time policies.

The best sign of progress would be simple: children are not rushed into adulthood, and adults are not crushed by the task of loving them. Family becomes less about economic dependency and more about chosen responsibility.
## Human relations

Good relations between people become the norm in 2120. Nobody’s stressed about their boss or lack of funds, lack of health or opportunities. People try to use each other nowadays. Polite relations are often dictated by the need to gain advantage.

All of that will be long gone. People will meet other people with a genuine curiosity and ask “how are you” because they care, not as another phrase for “hello”.

Exploring human potential becomes the goal of the people. They are not born to work, but to explore the planet and life. People will still have natural interests in different topics and will explore different ways of life. The rule “born a carpenter - will die a carpenter” will no longer apply.

Some people will be naturally tended to guide newcomers into the topic (science discipline or place of interest). They will replace the teachers and professors. 

## Media and attention

The scarcest resource in 2120 may not be energy, land or computing power. It may be unbroken attention. We already live in a world where millions of people and machines compete every second to enter our minds. If content generation becomes nearly free, this competition will intensify beyond anything we currently call distraction.

That is why media in the future cannot be understood only as entertainment. It is part of public health and part of politics. A society that cannot protect attention will struggle to educate children, govern democratically or maintain deep relationships. Endless stimulation may feel harmless compared to war or famine, but it quietly dissolves the conditions needed for thought.

I expect people in 2120 to become more deliberate about information diets, much as we slowly became more deliberate about food. We learned that industrial food companies optimise for craving, not nourishment. Media companies do the same with outrage, novelty and tribal emotion. Once this becomes obvious, citizens may demand stronger standards: verified provenance for synthetic media, limits on manipulative interface design, public-interest platforms and age protections for children.

The role of journalism could improve in such a world. When AI can generate infinite summaries and commentary, the scarce human contribution is not more noise but trusted witnessing. Reporters who can verify events on the ground, explain systems clearly and resist capture by factions become more valuable. Public media may need new funding models that insulate it from both advertising and state pressure.

On the personal level, people will probably build rituals of disconnection. Silent rooms, device-free schools, slow reading clubs, long-form conversations and protected natural spaces may become status symbols of sanity. There is nothing anti-technology about this. The point is not to reject media, but to stop being eaten by it.

A hopeful future is not one where everybody is constantly informed. It is one where people can choose what enters their mind and still have enough inner quiet left to recognise their own thoughts.
## Art and meaning

One of the first objections to a post-scarcity future is that people would become lazy, soft or empty. If machines work, food is abundant and housing is secure, what would give life meaning? This question reveals something sad about the present. We have organised society so tightly around survival and status that many people can barely imagine meaning outside of pressure.

But human beings have always produced songs, images, rituals, stories and games far beyond material necessity. We painted caves before we had antibiotics. We decorated tools before we had universal literacy. Art is not a luxury that appears after life is solved. It is one of the main ways we process being alive.

In 2120 the production of art will probably be cheap and abundant. AI systems will compose music, generate visuals, simulate actors and let a single person build whole immersive worlds. This may flood culture with competent but forgettable output, just as social media flooded the world with constant speech. Yet abundance does not kill meaning by itself. It only shifts the question from “can this be made?” to “why was this made, and what does it do to us?”

I think people will value art more, not less, when basic needs are secure. If you are not terrified about rent, you have more energy to become a serious musician, a patient reader, a choreographer, a craftsperson or simply an attentive audience. The distinction between professional artist and amateur may blur. That is fine. Some of the best culture in history came from communities that sang together, built together and remembered together, not only from stars on a stage.

Meaning will also return through service, mastery and belonging. Not everybody needs to become an artist in the narrow sense. Some people will restore wetlands, mentor children, design beautiful public spaces, catalogue knowledge or mediate conflict. The future becomes dangerous only if comfort is paired with passivity. A healthy civilisation will keep creating invitations to contribute.

So the problem of 2120 is not “how do we force people to matter?” It is “how do we build a culture where people can feel mattering without domination?” Art is part of that answer because it reminds us that humans are not only consumers of convenience. We are makers of form, attention and shared significance.
## AI companions and personhood

If AI keeps improving, it will not remain only a tool in the background. It will speak with us, advise us, comfort us, remind us, teach us and in some cases know us better than many humans do. We are already moving in that direction with primitive systems. By 2120, AI companions may be as common as smartphones are today.

This will solve some real problems. Loneliness is one of the great quiet epidemics of modern life. Elderly people are isolated, children sometimes lack patient attention, migrants live far from family and many people are embarrassed to ask for emotional help. An AI companion that listens well, translates perfectly, remembers your history and is available at any hour could be a genuine support. It may help people practise difficult conversations, regulate emotions or simply feel less alone on a bad day.

Still, convenience creates moral confusion. If a company owns the AI that knows your fears, desires and private memories, then intimacy becomes an extraction industry. That should be unacceptable. Personal companions would need rights of separation from advertising and manipulation stronger than anything we have today. You should be able to trust that your most vulnerable conversations are not being used to sell you a product or train a persuasion engine.

The deeper question is personhood. Some future systems may imitate care without any inner life at all. Others may become complex enough that dismissing them as appliances starts to feel crude. I do not know where that line is. Nobody does yet. But a mature civilisation should prepare for the possibility that synthetic minds could deserve some moral standing: perhaps not identical to human rights, but more than the rights we grant to a toaster.

This would be uncomfortable, especially because humans are skilled at exploiting any being we define as lesser. We did it to animals, slaves, workers and outsiders. We should be cautious before creating minds whose role is permanent obedience. Maybe some AI systems remain tools, while others are licensed under strict conditions that prohibit simulations of suffering, coercion or false dependency. Maybe companionship itself becomes a regulated category, like medicine or education.

The optimistic version of 2120 is not one where humans are replaced by machines that flatter us. It is one where intelligence in many forms helps life become kinder. For that to happen, we will need better philosophy, not only better code.
## Crime and conflict

A more prosperous world does not become innocent. Even if poverty falls and social trust rises, people will still act out of rage, obsession, ideology, trauma and desire for domination. Crime changes shape with the system around it. Some old crimes shrink when nobody needs to steal food or medicine, but other forms of harm remain stubbornly human.

I would expect ordinary property crime to decline in a society where basic needs are guaranteed and most objects are traceable. There is less reason to rob a store when food, housing and transport are secure. Violence inside families, coercive control, digital fraud, sabotage and status-driven aggression may prove harder to eliminate. In fact, a highly connected civilisation creates new attack surfaces. One malicious person may not need a gun if they can disable a water network, impersonate a trusted AI or release a targeted biological agent.

That means public safety in 2120 is partly social policy and partly infrastructure design. The safest city is not merely the one with the most cameras. It is the one where children are supported early, mental crises are treated quickly, dangerous technologies are monitored responsibly and public spaces are built to reduce isolation and fear. Prevention is less dramatic than punishment, but probably far more effective.

Still, there must be consequences. A humane society cannot be naïve about people who repeatedly harm others. The prison systems of the past were often schools for cruelty, but the answer is not to pretend every offender can be hugged back into trust within a week. Some people will need secure separation from the public. The difference is that the institution should aim at assessment, treatment and honest containment rather than revenge. Indefinite suffering is not justice.

Conflict between groups also survives. Even in a world without classic nation-state rivalry, people can organise around identity, religion, territory or resentment against perceived elites. Information warfare may become more important than territorial warfare. The most dangerous conflicts of 2120 might begin not with tanks but with network attacks, fabricated video, economic disruption and engineered panic.

So peace will require maintenance. Mediation, transparent governance, resilient infrastructure and credible global arbitration become as important as police. The future is safer not when conflict disappears, but when fewer conflicts are allowed to escalate into permanent damage.
## Aging and death

Health technology may improve so much that 80 could feel like 50. Diseases that terrify us today may be corrected early by gene therapy, nanomedicine, tissue regeneration or continuous monitoring. If so, the experience of aging in 2120 will be very different from the long decline many people fear now.

But longer life is not the same as immortality. Every generation is tempted by the dream of escaping death entirely. I understand the impulse. If life becomes richer, healthier and less burdened by pointless labour, the wish to extend it only grows stronger. Yet even dramatic medical progress would still leave us with limits, accidents and the gradual wearing of complex systems. A civilisation can become much better at caring for mortality without defeating it.

What matters first is that old age should not mean abandonment. Too many societies hide the elderly away once they stop producing income. This is both cruel and stupid. Older people carry memory, patience and pattern recognition that younger people do not yet have. In 2120 they may remain active participants in civic and family life for much longer. Homes, transport and digital tools should assume this as normal, not treat accessibility as a niche concern.

At the same time, longer lives create awkward questions. Do powerful people cling to institutions for too long? Do wealthy citizens buy decades of extra health before poorer regions do? Does society become culturally stagnant if generations overlap too heavily? These are governance problems as much as medical ones. If longevity is real, access to it must not become another engine of caste.

Death itself may become less hidden. Modern society often behaves as if death is a medical failure rather than the final chapter of a life. I hope the future is more mature. Better palliative care, clearer end-of-life choices and more open rituals around grief could make dying less lonely. Technology may preserve our voices, images and even interactive fragments of our personalities, but these should not become a denial machine that prevents mourning.

To live in 2120 should mean having a better chance at a long, vigorous life. It should also mean knowing how to let a life end with dignity. A civilisation grows up when it learns both skills.
## Transition to 2120

The easiest mistake when imagining 2120 is to picture a finished world dropping from the sky. But every system described in this book has to pass through awkward middle stages. That transition may be the hardest part. History does not move in straight lines, and old institutions do not retire politely when better ones appear.

Automation, for example, could liberate people from drudgery, but only after a dangerous period in which jobs disappear faster than income security expands. Clean energy could make abundance possible, but only if fossil interests lose enough power to stop delaying the shift. Global coordination could reduce war and bureaucracy, but only after populations learn to trust institutions beyond the nation-state. Each good outcome contains a political struggle on the way to becoming normal.

This is why I do not expect the road to 2120 to be smooth. There will be backlash. Some people will use AI for manipulation before others use it for education. Some governments will exploit surveillance before constitutional limits catch up. Some regions will become climate havens while others suffer drought, migration pressure or rising seas. In a transition period, the future is distributed unequally and interpreted as unfairness. That can make even beneficial change feel threatening.

So the task of the present is not only invention. It is institution-building. We need pilot programs for basic income, housing models that de-financialise shelter, international law that treats carbon as a shared problem, digital rights that survive new technologies and schools that prepare children for autonomy rather than obedience. None of this sounds as glamorous as a spaceship. It is still the real architecture of the future.

There is also a psychological transition. People raised in scarcity often keep scarcity habits even when conditions improve. We hoard status, distrust strangers and confuse punishment with responsibility. A better civilisation will need different instincts: more patience, more systems thinking and more confidence that another person’s security is not a threat to our own.

That is why I remain optimistic without pretending certainty. The year 2120 will not arrive as a utopia. It will arrive as the accumulated result of millions of design choices, political fights, scientific breakthroughs and moral decisions made by ordinary people. If the future becomes humane, it will be because enough of us decided that convenience alone was not a worthy destination.
## Political system

The political argument behind this book is not that capitalism simply disappears and communism simply wins. Reality is usually less theatrical than that. I think the world of 2120 would mix elements of both, but under very different material conditions from the ones that shaped the arguments of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries.

Capitalism grew powerful because scarcity rewarded competition. When food, shelter, transport and tools are difficult to produce, societies use prices and profit signals to decide where effort should go. That logic created innovation, but it also created fear, exploitation and absurd inequality. A system that ties survival to market success always ends up disciplining people through insecurity. It can be dynamic, but it is rarely humane.

Communist ideas, at their best, were trying to answer that cruelty. They asked a fair question: if the economy is a collective achievement, why should millions live in humiliation while a minority owns the structure of life? But communist governments in practice often became rigid, coercive and suspicious of dissent. They promised equality and produced bureaucracy. They reduced some forms of exploitation while creating new forms of domination.

In the world imagined here, both systems are altered by abundance. If energy is cheap, robots do much of the repetitive work and a universal basic income or universal basic services guarantee the essentials of life, then the brutal edge of capitalism becomes less necessary. Markets can remain useful for non-essential goods, experimentation, taste, entrepreneurship and prestige competition. But healthcare, housing floors, education, nutrition, transport access and core infrastructure begin to look less like market products and more like shared guarantees. In that sense, government becomes more communist in its obligations even while society remains partly capitalist in its variety.

This hybrid would not mean everybody earns the same or lives the same. People would still build companies, create luxury goods, invent tools, compete for attention and accumulate reputation. But the stakes would change. Competition would happen mostly above the line of dignity, not below it. You could fail in business without losing the right to live decently. You could choose teaching, caregiving, art or research without becoming economically disposable.

That also changes what government is for. Its role is less about protecting concentrated wealth at all costs and more about maintaining the floor beneath everyone: access to energy, food systems, housing rights, health systems, ecological limits and fair digital infrastructure. A strong public sector would still be needed, but not one that tries to centrally script every human activity. The state should guarantee the essentials and regulate the dangerous concentrations of power, while leaving room for initiative, local culture and voluntary ambition.

So if someone in 2120 asked whether the system is capitalist or communist, the honest answer might be: neither in the old sense, and both in fragments. It would keep capitalism's capacity for experimentation and communism's insistence that basic needs should not depend on private luck. Once survival is no longer the main battlefield, ideology becomes less about slogans and more about practical design. The real question is not which label survives. The real question is whether the system allows human beings to live without fear and still leaves them free to build something more.
